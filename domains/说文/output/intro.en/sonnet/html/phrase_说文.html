🌸 Phrase 说文

When you learn a new word in any language, you probably ask someone to explain it to you — what does it mean, where does it come from? That simple act of explaining language through language is exactly what the phrase 说文 captures.

说文 (shuō wén) pairs two characters that you will meet again and again. 说 means to speak or to explain — it carries the sense of putting something into words. 文 means writing, text, or the written word — it points to language in its recorded, visible form. Put them together and you get the idea of explaining writing: using spoken or written words to shed light on how characters work.

Here is the simple rule: in Chinese, two-character phrases often work like this — the first character acts as a verb (what you do), and the second character names the thing you are doing it to. 说 + 文 = explain + writing = explaining writing.

A famous ancient dictionary carries exactly this name — its compilers spent years speaking and writing about characters, tracing each one back to its roots. But you do not need to know that history to use the phrase. Any time a teacher points to a character on the board and starts unpacking its meaning, that teacher is doing 说文.

Try it yourself: Look at any two-character phrase you already know. Can you name what each character contributes? That act of noticing — right there — is your own small moment of 说文.

Payoff 🎯

Think about the last time someone explained a word to you by breaking it apart — "photograph means light-writing in Greek." Suddenly the word clicked, and every other word with photo- became a little easier. That is exactly what 说文 does for Chinese.

Picture a master teacher sitting with you, pointing at a character and saying, "Here is what this shape is telling you." The phrase 说文 — explain the writing — names that very act. 说 means to speak or to explain; 文 means writing, character, and by extension the whole world of culture carried in words. Put them together and you have the idea of making writing speak.

The simple rule is this: 说文 is the moment a character stops being a picture you memorize and becomes a story you understand. Once you can explain a character — where its parts come from, what they mean together — you own it in a way that sticks.

Try it yourself: take the character 明, meaning bright. Can you explain it? The sun 日 beside the moon 月 — two sources of light, side by side. That little act of explanation is 说文 in action.

This is the natural endpoint of the textbook because every skill you have built — recognizing radicals, reading components, tracing meaning — has been preparation for this one move: opening your mouth (or your mind) and explaining the writing.
